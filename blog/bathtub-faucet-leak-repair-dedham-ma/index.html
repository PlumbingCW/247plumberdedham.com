Bathtub Faucet Leak Repair in Dedham, MA

A dripping tub spout wastes water around the clock. Identify the valve type first, then the repair is usually a small part.

Bathtub faucet leak repair in Dedham, MA starts with one question: how many handles do you have? One handle that moves in an arc usually hides a cartridge. Two handles that screw down usually hide compression stems. A drip from the spout after you pull up the knob can be the diverter. Each fails for a different reason, so a few minutes of identification saves a wasted trip to the hardware store.

Know the three valve types.

Cartridge valves sit behind a single lever or a pair of handles that turn only a quarter or half turn. A rubber-sealed cartridge slides or rotates to control flow. When the seals wear or scale builds up, water bypasses the cartridge and drips from the spout.

Compression valves are the older style. Two handles turn many times to close. Inside, a rubber washer presses against a seat. The washer wears down, or the seat pits, and the faucet drips. This type is common in older bathrooms.

Diverter valves send water to the tub spout or up to the shower. A tub spout with a pull-up knob has the diverter in the spout itself. A three-handle set has a separate center handle. If water keeps coming out of the showerhead while the tub runs, or the shower is weak, the diverter is the likely suspect.

Which leak are you chasing?.

Where the water shows up Likely cause Typical fix.
Drips from the spout when off Worn cartridge, washer or seat Replace cartridge or washer, resurface seat.
Drip around the handle Packing, O-ring or loose stem Tighten, replace O-ring.
Water from showerhead while tub fills Worn diverter Replace diverter spout or valve.
Drip behind the wall or stain on ceiling below Supply joint or valve body Plumber, may need access.
Handle hard to move, weak flow Mineral buildup Clean or replace cartridge.

Run your hand along the wall under the tub if you can reach it, and check the ceiling below for stains. A leak inside the wall will not show at the spout, and waiting makes the damage worse.

The first step: find your shutoffs.

Before you take anything apart, stop the water. Many tubs have no local shutoffs behind the valve, which is common in older homes. If you see an access panel on the other side of the wall, often in a closet or a hallway, look for small stop valves there.

If you find none, close the main shutoff, usually near the water meter. Open another faucet to drain pressure. Plug the tub drain with a rag so a dropped screw does not vanish.

If the main valve will not turn, stop. Do not use a wrench on an old valve, because it can crack. This is a good time to call the 24/7 line.

How the repair goes.

Pry off the cap on the handle, remove the screw and pull the handle. A cartridge usually comes out with a retaining clip or nut, then pulls straight out with a puller or pliers. Take it to the store. Parts vary by brand, and the wrong cartridge does not seal.

On a compression faucet, unscrew the stem, replace the washer and the O-ring, and check the seat inside the valve with your fingertip. A rough or grooved seat will chew up new washers. A seat wrench or dresser tool can resurface it. If you do not have the tool, the plumber does.

For the diverter spout, look for a set screw under the spout or slip it off by turning counterclockwise. Many tubs use a slip-on spout with a small hex key. Replace the spout, and the leak at the diverter often goes away.

Use plumber's grease on O-rings and rubber seals. Do not overtighten plastic parts.

What Dedham homeowners should know.

The Dedham-Westwood Water District's 2025 water quality report lists the water's hardness at an average of about 6.0 grains per gallon, with a range from 4.3 to 7.8. That is moderately hard water. Over time, calcium and magnesium collect inside cartridges and on seats, which makes handles stiff and seals fail sooner than they would in soft water.

If you replace a cartridge and the new one starts dripping within a few years, scale is a likely contributor.

You can slow the damage by wiping the spout and handle, soaking the old cartridge in vinegar to see if buildup is the problem, and replacing it before it fails. A whole-house conditioner is a bigger project and a separate decision.

The same report says the District adjusts pH to keep water from being corrosive to pipes, and adds orthophosphate for corrosion control. That is helpful background, but it does not protect the rubber and brass in a worn faucet. Treat the faucet as a wear item.

Massachusetts requires no permit to fix a leaky faucet. Replacing the valve in the wall, however, is plumbing work, and permits are issued only to licensed plumbers.

When to stop and call a plumber.

Hand this one off if you see any of these:

  • The handle will not stop the water, even with the cartridge out..
  • You cannot remove the cartridge or stem without damaging the valve body..
  • The valve is corroded, cracked or loose in the wall..
  • There is a stain on the ceiling below or soft drywall near the tub..
  • The shower diverter fails again soon after replacement..
  • Your tub has a single handle and the wall behind it is tile, so access needs a cut..

A valve body replacement usually means opening the wall from behind or through the tile. Ask the plumber whether the repair can be done from an access panel before you agree to cut tile.

Cost drivers, without guessing a number.

There is no universal price for a tub faucet repair. A cartridge swap is a short visit. A valve body replacement behind tile is a bigger job. The drivers are access, valve brand and whether parts are in stock, whether the old valve is corroded, and whether the finish needs matching. If you want a firm number, ask for a clear price before work starts.

Think about the whole bathroom too. If the shower valve is leaking, other fixtures nearby are the same age. A plumber may suggest replacing both while the wall is open. See faucet repair for the range of fixtures covered, and leak detection if you suspect water is traveling inside the wall.
