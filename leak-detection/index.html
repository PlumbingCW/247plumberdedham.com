Water on a ceiling, a warm patch on the floor, or a meter that keeps moving is urgent, even when the damage looks small. Emergency leak detection in Dedham, MA means finding where pressurized water is escaping, then fixing only that spot.

Our 24/7 emergency dispatch sends licensed plumbers, and same-day appointments are offered when available. While you wait, shut off the main valve, switch off power to wet areas, and move belongings out of the way.

Water coming through a ceiling or wall right now.

Treat an active ceiling or wall leak as an emergency. Water follows framing and wiring, so the wet spot you see is often several feet from the real source. Shut the main valve, then open the lowest faucet in the house to drain the lines.

If a light fixture is wet or the ceiling is bulging, stay out of that room and turn off the breaker for it. A bulging ceiling can hold a lot of water and may drop without warning, so keep people and pets out from under it.

Ceiling leaks usually come from one of three places: a supply line above, a drain line above, or the roof. A plumber can separate the first two by shutting the supply off and watching whether the dripping stops. If it stops, the leak is on a supply line. If it keeps going after a flush or a shower, it is likely a drain.

Your meter is still running with everything off.

A meter that keeps moving after every tap, appliance, and ice maker is idle is the clearest sign of a leak on your side of the meter. The Dedham-Westwood Water District (DWWD) supplies water in Dedham, and its leak guidance says a higher bill is either caused by a leak or by a change in how much water you use. A running meter at zero demand rules out the second cause.

Test it yourself before you call:

  1. Turn off every fixture, the dishwasher, the washing machine, and the ice maker..
  2. Find the meter and note the reading, or take a photo of the dial..
  3. Wait 30 to 60 minutes without using any water..
  4. Check again. Any movement means water is escaping somewhere..

This is also the right moment to say what we cannot tell you. DWWD's billing-adjustment policy for leaks is not something we can state, so call the District office at 781-329-7090 and ask about it directly. Keep your meter photos and the plumber's invoice, because they document when the leak started and when it was fixed.

Run the toilet dye test first.

Toilets are the most common quiet leak. DWWD recommends a simple dye test: drop food coloring or a dye tablet into the tank, do not flush, and wait 15 minutes. If color shows in the bowl, the flapper is leaking and water is running into the drain all day.

DWWD says it gives out free leak tablets at its office at 50 Elm St, so you do not need to buy anything. A worn flapper is a cheap fix, and clearing it up first makes every other test easier to read. If the dye stays in the tank and the meter still moves, the leak is somewhere else.

Room-by-room checklist before you call.

  • Look under every sink for damp cabinet floors, swollen wood, or a musty smell..
  • Check the washing machine hoses and the shutoff valves behind it..
  • Look at the base of the water heater for drips or rust stains..
  • Press on ceilings below bathrooms for soft or discolored spots..
  • Watch the ice maker line and refrigerator water line for moisture..
  • Check outdoor hose bibs and any irrigation valve boxes for standing water..
  • Listen at night for a faint hiss near walls or the water heater..

Signs the leak is on the service line.

If every fixture checks out and the meter still runs, suspect the pipe between the meter and the house. DWWD lists the warning signs: low pressure, discolored water, water bubbling up in the yard, or an overflowing meter pit.

Low pressure at every faucet at once points toward the service line rather than one fixture. Soggy grass that stays green and wet in dry weather is another clue. Because DWWD owns the main and the street side, ask the District where its responsibility ends and yours begins before you hire any digging. Our water line repair page explains the repair choices once the leak is located.

Under-house and crawlspace leaks.

Homes with a crawlspace or an unfinished basement give a plumber something useful: a clear look at the pipes. Look for wet insulation, mineral crust on joints, and drip marks on the dirt or slab below. A hissing sound near the water heater or main shutoff is worth reporting.

In neighborhoods the Town describes as flood prone, such as Manor, water in a basement is not always plumbing. Groundwater can seep through the floor after heavy rain. A meter test settles it: if the meter is still when everything is off and water keeps appearing, the source is probably outside the pipes. If the meter moves, the source is probably a pipe.

If your house or an addition sits on a slab, the same meter test applies. Slab leaks hide under flooring, so listen for running water you cannot explain and feel for warm spots on the floor, which can point to a leaking hot water line.

Buried line leaks.

A buried leak does not show up on a ceiling. It shows up as a high bill, soft ground, or low pressure. Plumbers use acoustic listening gear and pressure testing to narrow down the location, then confirm before anyone digs.

Massachusetts requires you to call Dig Safe at 811 at least 72 business hours before excavation, and that applies to homeowners too. Municipal water and sewer lines may not be marked, so ask your plumber how they will locate the line. When you call, ask whether the plumber brings listening equipment and how they confirm a buried leak before digging.

Irrigation system leaks.

Sprinkler systems leak at valve boxes, risers, and the connection to the house. A broken head sprays visibly. A cracked underground pipe may only show as a soggy patch or a pressure drop in the zone.

Test each zone one at a time, watching for pooling. Most irrigation leaks are quick repairs once found. Check the backflow preventer too, since the plumbing code requires a backflow preventer or vacuum breaker on hose connections, and a failed one can leak or let dirty water back into your lines.

Pool, spa, yard, and meter-box leaks.

A pool or spa that loses more than evaporation explains should be tested by marking the water line, then comparing a day with the pump on to a day with it off. If the loss continues with the pump off, the shell or a fitting is the suspect. If it stops, the plumbing is.

Water standing in the yard or in the meter box is its own signal. DWWD names an overflowing meter pit as a service-line warning sign, so call rather than pumping it out and ignoring it.

How plumbers find hidden leaks.

Good leak detection starts with the cheapest tests and moves to equipment only when needed. Here is what each method can and cannot see.

Method What it finds Limit.
Meter test Whether any leak exists Not where it is.
Pressure test Leaks in a section of line Needs the section isolated.
Acoustic listening Pressurized leaks in pipe Hard in plastic or noisy areas.
Moisture meter Wet drywall and framing Shows wetness, not the source.
Thermal camera Temperature patterns in walls Cannot see through everything.
Camera on a drain Drain-side cracks Does not find supply leaks.

No single tool is certain, so a plumber should combine two or three and explain which one pointed where. Ask whether they will open the wall at the smallest spot possible.

Repair options and honest trade-offs.

Once a leak is located, you usually choose between a spot repair and replacing a section. A spot repair is faster and cheaper but leaves older pipe beside it. If the pipe around the leak looks corroded or thin, a longer replacement may save a second visit.

The Town's Master Plan says 74 percent of Dedham houses were built before 1978, and older homes often carry a mix of copper, galvanized steel, cast iron, and plastic pipe, so a second weak spot nearby is not unusual. Ask the plumber to inspect adjoining pipe before closing the wall. If the same house keeps springing leaks, talk about repiping rather than paying for repeated patches.

What to do while you wait.
